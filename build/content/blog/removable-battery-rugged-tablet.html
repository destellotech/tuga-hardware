What Removable Actually Means

Most consumer tablets and phones have the battery glued or screwed inside a sealed shell. When it wears out, the device goes to a repairer or in a drawer. A removable battery is a pack with its own casing and contacts that sits in a bay behind a latched or screwed cover. You take it out and put another one in, the way you would with a cordless drill.

On a rugged device the cover is part of the sealing. It has a gasket and it latches or screws down, so the IP rating holds with the battery fitted and the cover closed properly. That is the one habit to learn: close the cover fully and check the latch every time, because an open or half closed cover is an open door for water and dust whatever the rating on the spec sheet says.

Removable does not always mean hot swappable. We do not list hot swap on any Tuga device, so save your work and power down before you swap, and treat anything better as a bonus rather than a promise.

Three Reasons It Matters

1. A Swap Beats a Charger on a Long Shift

Mains power is rare on a site and awkward in a van. You can top up from a 12V DC Car Charger (£26) between jobs, and most people do, but charging takes time and the device has to be in the vehicle while it happens. A charged spare takes seconds to fit and the device is back at full with no downtime at all. For a delivery round, a long day of callouts, a stocktake or a shift pattern where one device passes between two people, the spare is the difference between finishing the day and hunting for a plug socket at four o'clock.

We cannot tell you how many hours any of our devices run between charges, because it depends on screen brightness, how much the radios are working and what the apps are doing. Our guide to making a tablet battery last a full shift goes through the settings that stretch a charge. A removable battery is the fallback for when the settings are not enough.

2. A Worn Battery Becomes a Part, Not a Reason to Replace the Device

Every lithium battery loses capacity with use. That is normal, it is not a defect, and it is the most common reason a working tablet gets replaced before the rest of it has failed. Our guide on how long a rugged tablet should last covers the other things that end a device's working life, but battery wear is the one most people meet first.

With a sealed battery, a tired cell means a repair bill, a repairer who may not want the job, or a new device. With a removable one, it means fitting a fresh pack and carrying on. The screen, the seals, the processor and your software setup all keep going. We do not list a spare or replacement battery as an accessory on the site at the moment, so if you are buying with this in mind, ask us about availability and price for the model you are considering before you rely on it.

The warranty position is worth knowing too. The 12 month warranty included with every device covers defects, and the paid 3-Year Warranty (£65 per device on the A6, A8 and A10, £79 on the W8 and W10, £89 on the WH6) covers battery defects as well, but neither covers normal capacity loss. A battery that gradually holds less charge over years of use is wear, not a fault. A removable battery is the practical answer to wear; the warranty is the answer to a fault.

3. Spares Give You Options

A spare can live in the van, a toolbag or the office drawer, and a team can keep charged packs on a bench for anyone to grab. With a sealed device, the only spare is a second device.

Cold Changes the Arithmetic

Batteries deliver less in the cold, and a device that comfortably lasted a summer day can run short in January. The A6 is rated for -20°C to 50°C, and the A10, WH6 and W8 for -20°C to 60°C; the A8 and W10 do not list an operating temperature, so treat them with care in a frost. Our cold weather battery guide covers why this happens and what to do about it. The point for this guide is that a spare battery kept warm, in an inside pocket rather than the glovebox, is a better cold weather plan than a bigger battery left out in the van.

Looking After Spare Batteries

A spare pack is a lithium battery, so the usual common sense applies.

  1. Store it part charged, not full and not flat. A pack left completely empty for months may not recover.
  2. Rotate the spares so they wear evenly rather than one dying while the other sits unused.
  3. Keep it out of heat. A dashboard in July is the worst place for a battery. A door pocket or the office is better. Our guide to using a tablet in summer has more on heat.
  4. Protect the contacts. Keep a loose spare in a small bag or the pocket of the Rugged Carry Case (£34, fits the A8, A10, W8 and W10) rather than loose with keys and screws.
  5. Check the cover gasket when you swap. Grit on the seal is how water gets in. Wipe it and make sure the latch closes fully.
  6. Dispose of old packs properly. Lithium batteries do not go in household waste. Most councils and many retailers take them.
