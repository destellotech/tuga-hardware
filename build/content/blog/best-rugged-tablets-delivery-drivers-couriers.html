Start With Who Chooses the App

A delivery device is only as useful as the app it runs, and in this line of work you often do not pick the app.

  • You drive for a carrier. The app is fixed; your job is to buy a device that runs it. Courier apps differ in which platforms they support, so ask your carrier before you buy.
  • You run your own rounds. You choose a proof of delivery or route app, so check which platforms it supports; if it runs on Android, that is the cheaper route.
  • A warehouse system sets the rules. Some stock and dispatch systems still run a Windows client on the handheld. That points at a Windows handheld with a scan engine, at a very different price.

We cannot promise that any third party app will run on a given device, so buy one, install your app and use it for a day before ordering for the team. You have 30 days from delivery to return a device in its original condition and packaging; you pay return carriage unless it is faulty or we sent the wrong item. Proof of Delivery: Photo, Signature or Both

Proof of delivery often means a photo, a signature or both, uploaded before you are back in the cab. Each asks something different of the device.

The Doorstep Photo

A photo is only proof if you can see the parcel and the door in it. In winter, some of a round can happen in the dark, which is where the camera spec matters. The A6 has a 64MP night vision camera alongside a 50MP main camera. The A8 has a 13MP rear camera, the WH6 an 8MP autofocus rear camera. All three will photograph a parcel in daylight; only the A6 lists a camera built for low light. For the upload, the A6 has 5G and the A8 and WH6 have 4G LTE; any of them will do it, but 5G finishes sooner where there is coverage.

The Signature

A 6.5 or 6.78 inch screen is plenty for a finger signature, so a signature is not a reason to carry a tablet. Glove mode is listed on the A6 and A8, which helps when the person signing has winter gloves on; the WH6 does not list it. An IP68 rating means the device survives rain, not that a wet screen reads a wet finger cleanly, so a wipe on a sleeve is still part of the job.

If your customers prefer a pen, the Capacitive Stylus at £14.99 is listed for every Tuga device except the A6, so on the A6 plan for finger signatures.

Scanning: Count Your Scans Before You Pay for an Engine

Delivery scanning happens in two places: every parcel at loading, which can be a handful or a few hundred, and one or two at each drop. The load decides whether a built-in scan engine is worth the money.

The A6 has no scan engine. It scans with the camera through an app, which is fine at the door and slower for the load. If your carrier's app does the load scan on the camera anyway, the A6 does the job. If you are scanning every parcel in a cold trailer, dedicated engines are generally quicker at each scan and cope better with scuffed labels than a camera does. Our barcode scanner guide compares camera, Bluetooth scanner and built-in engine in detail.

In our range the scan engines are on the Windows devices. The WH6 with Barcode Scanner is the pocket sized one: a 6.5 inch Windows 11 handheld with an integrated 2D scanner, listed as reading all major barcode formats, at £779, which is £80 more than the standard WH6. The Tuga W8 with 2D Scanner at £699 is the 8 inch tablet version for a depot desk. Some courier labels carry 2D codes, and a 2D engine reads 1D stripes too.

Choosing the WH6 for its scanner means choosing Windows, so run your software on Windows 11 before you commit. The middle path for a small firm on Android apps is an A6 per driver plus a Bluetooth scanner at the depot for the morning load (check the scanner pairs with your delivery app before you buy).

Pocket Size and the Walk to the Door

Parcels take both hands, so the device needs a home that is not your hand: a pocket or holster, not a cab mount.

The A6 is 6.78 inches and 441g, with Gorilla Glass 5 and a screen protector already applied in the box. The standard WH6 is 6.5 inches and 470g, at 183.1 x 88.6 x 24.1mm. WH6 specs here are those of the standard model; the scanner version is listed as the standard WH6 plus the integrated 2D scanner, so confirm weight and battery with us before ordering. The A6 and WH6 both fit the Belt Holster at £22, which takes devices up to 6.8 inches, for the walk to the door. For the cab, both also fit the Car Phone Mount at £17, a suction mount usable one handed with gloves on. The A8 at 8 inches is a cab device: vehicle mountable, with the Vehicle Mounting Dock at £109 to lock it to the dashboard and charge it, but at 680g it is a lot to carry to a front door all day. The full list is on our accessories page.

Drops are the other half of pocket life. The A6 and WH6 are both rated MIL-STD-810H, and the WH6 lists a 1.5 metre drop spec. The A6 is sealed to IP68 and IP69K, so it survives a puddle and a jet wash. The WH6 is IP65: rain and water jets, not immersion, so keep it out of the bucket. All-Day Battery: Numbers and Habits

A round keeps navigation, mobile data and the camera running together, so compare battery size before you compare anything else. The figures here are far apart: the A6 has 10600mAh with 33W fast charging, the A8 10000mAh with 18W, and the WH6 4000mAh, removable, with USB-C PD fast charging and a pogo pin for the dock.

The WH6 figure is the trade-off for Windows and a scan engine in a pocket sized body. The standard WH6 battery is removable, and the WH6 Charging Cradle at £79 drops the handheld onto pogo pins at the end of the day. We do not list a spare battery, so ask us whether one is available before you plan a shift around swapping.

The 12V DC Car Charger at £26 fits every device, with a USB-C output on a 2 metre cable for topping up between drops. An A10, W8 or W10 that returns to the depot each night can live on the Charging Dock at £49.99. All three are rated to operate from -20°C to 60°C, but a cold battery still delivers less than its rating. Our winter battery guide covers what happens and the one charging mistake to avoid.

The Screen in the Cab

If the same device is the route screen on the dash, brightness matters more than resolution. The A8 has the highest listed brightness of the three, at 800 nits, against 560 for the A6 and 400 for the WH6. Low winter sun through a windscreen will find out a 400 nit screen, so if the WH6 is also your sat nav, expect to angle it. All three list GPS and GLONASS, and the A6 adds BeiDou.

Locking a Device Full of Addresses

A delivery device holds names and addresses for everyone on the round, so it needs a lock that gets used. The A6 lists a fingerprint sensor and face unlock. The A8 and WH6 do not list either, so on those use a PIN or password. Set a lock on day one, and use whatever remote lock and wipe your account or device management software offers. Our product pages do not list voice calling, so if ringing customers from the device matters, ask us before you order.

Which One, by How You Work

  • Self-employed courier on a carrier app: Tuga A6, £299. One device, in the pocket, and the camera does the scans if your carrier's app supports it.
  • Small firm running its own rounds on Android or web apps: an A6 per driver, and an A8 at £379 for whoever plans routes from the cab.
  • High scan volume every morning, or a Windows warehouse system: WH6 with Barcode Scanner, £779, plus the cradle. The device alone is £480 more than the A6 (£779 against £299), before the £79 cradle, so it earns its place only when scan volume or Windows software demands it.
  • Large items, two person crews, device stays in the van: an A8 in a Vehicle Mounting Dock, with an A6 for whoever walks to the door.

If your carrier issues a device, you do not need to buy one. If you do a handful of drops a day for a local shop, the phone in your pocket with a decent case will probably see you through.
