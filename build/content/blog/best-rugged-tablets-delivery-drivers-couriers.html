Start With Who Chooses the App

A delivery device is only as useful as the app it runs, and in this line of work you often do not pick the app.

  • You drive for a carrier. The app is fixed; your job is to buy a device that runs it. Courier apps differ in which platforms they support, so ask your carrier before you buy.
  • You run your own rounds. You choose a proof of delivery or route app, so check which platforms it supports; if it runs on Android, that is the cheaper route.
  • A warehouse system sets the rules. Some stock and dispatch systems still run a Windows client on the handheld. That points at a Windows handheld with a scan engine, at a very different price.

We cannot promise that any third party app will run on a given device, so buy one, install your app and use it for a day before ordering for the team. You have 30 days from delivery to return a device in its original condition and packaging; you pay return carriage unless it is faulty or we sent the wrong item. Proof of Delivery: Photo, Signature or Both

Proof of delivery often means a photo, a signature or both, uploaded before you are back in the cab. Each asks something different of the device.

The Doorstep Photo

A photo is only proof if you can see the parcel and the door in it. In winter, some of a round can happen in the dark, which is where the camera spec matters. The A6 and A8 both have 13MP rear cameras with a flash, the A8's with autofocus, and the WH6 an 8MP autofocus rear camera. All three will photograph a parcel in daylight; only the A6 and A8 list a flash, which helps on a dark doorstep. For the upload, all three have 4G LTE.

The Signature

A 6 or 6.5 inch screen is plenty for a finger signature, so a signature is not a reason to carry a tablet. None of the A6, A8 or WH6 lists a glove mode, so expect the person signing to take a winter glove off; the A6's side scan keys do work with gloves on. An IP67 or IP68 rating means the device survives rain, not that a wet screen reads a wet finger cleanly, so a wipe on a sleeve is still part of the job.

If your customers prefer a pen, the Capacitive Stylus at £14.99 is listed for every Tuga device except the A6, so on the A6 plan for finger signatures.

Scanning: Count Your Scans Before You Pay for an Engine

Delivery scanning happens in two places: every parcel at loading, which can be a handful or a few hundred, and one or two at each drop. The load decides whether a built-in scan engine is worth the money.

The A6 has a Zebra 2D scan engine built in, with two side scan keys, so the morning load and the doorstep scan are both done on the device in your pocket. Dedicated engines are generally quicker at each scan and cope better with scuffed labels than a phone camera does, and a 2D engine reads 1D stripes too, which matters when some courier labels carry 2D codes. If you drive for a carrier, check that its app works with the A6's scanner before you buy for a team. Our barcode scanner guide compares camera, Bluetooth scanner and built-in engine in detail.

The other scan engines in our range are on the Windows devices. The WH6 with Barcode Scanner is the Windows handheld: a 6.5 inch Windows 11 device with an integrated 2D scanner, listed as reading all major barcode formats, at £779, which is £80 more than the standard WH6. The Tuga W8 with 2D Scanner at £699 is the 8 inch tablet version for a depot desk.

Choosing the WH6 for its scanner means choosing Windows, so it only makes sense if your warehouse or dispatch software needs Windows; run that software on Windows 11 before you commit. For a small firm on Android or web apps, an A6 per driver covers the load and the doorstep on one device, for £430 less per driver than the WH6 with Barcode Scanner.

Pocket Size and the Walk to the Door

Parcels take both hands, so the device needs a home that is not your hand: a pocket or holster, not a cab mount.

The A6 is 6 inches, 277g and 164 x 77.6 x 16mm, with a hand strap in the box. The standard WH6 is 6.5 inches and 470g, at 183.1 x 88.6 x 24.1mm. WH6 specs here are those of the standard model; the scanner version is listed as the standard WH6 plus the integrated 2D scanner, so confirm weight and battery with us before ordering. The A6 and WH6 both fit the Belt Holster at £22, which takes devices up to 6.8 inches, for the walk to the door. For the cab, both also fit the Car Phone Mount at £17, a suction mount usable one handed with gloves on. The A8 at 8 inches is a cab device: vehicle mountable, with VESA mount points that take the Vehicle Mounting Dock at £109 to lock it to the dashboard and charge it. No weight is listed for it, but an 8 inch tablet is a lot to hold alongside parcels on every walk to a front door. The full list is on our accessories page.

Drops are the other half of pocket life. The A6 and WH6 both list a 1.5 metre drop rating, and the WH6 is also rated MIL-STD-810H, which the A6 does not list. The A6 is sealed to IP67, so it survives a puddle or a short dunk. The WH6 is IP65: rain and water jets, not immersion, so keep it out of the bucket. Neither rating covers a pressure washer, so wipe them clean rather than hosing them down. The A8 lists no drop rating at all, which is one more reason to leave it locked in the cab. All-Day Battery: Numbers and Habits

A round keeps navigation, mobile data and the camera running together, so compare battery size before you compare anything else. The figures here are far apart: the A8 has 8000mAh, removable, the A6 5050mAh, removable, and the WH6 4000mAh, removable, with USB-C PD fast charging and a pogo pin for the dock. No charging speed is listed for the A6 or the A8.

The handheld figures are the trade-off for a scan engine in a pocket sized body. Both batteries are removable, which is the answer to a long round: a charged spare swapped in at lunch. The A6 comes with one battery in the box and we do not list a spare for either handheld, so ask us whether one is available before you plan a shift around swapping. The WH6 Charging Cradle at £79 drops the WH6 onto pogo pins at the end of the day.

The 12V DC Car Charger at £26 fits every device, with a USB-C output on a 2 metre cable for topping up between drops. An A10, W8 or W10 that returns to the depot each night can live on the Charging Dock at £49.99. The WH6 is rated to operate from -20°C to 60°C and the A6 from -20°C to 50°C; no operating temperature is listed for the A8. Whatever the rating, a cold battery delivers less than its capacity. The Screen in the Cab

If the same device is the route screen on the dash, brightness matters more than resolution. The A8 has the highest listed brightness of the three, at 1000 nits, against 400 for the WH6. No brightness figure is listed for the A6, so try it in your own cab before you rely on it as a sat nav. Low winter sun through a windscreen will find out a 400 nit screen, so if the WH6 is also your sat nav, expect to angle it. All three list GPS and GLONASS, and the A6 and A8 add BeiDou and Galileo.

Locking a Device Full of Addresses

A delivery device holds names and addresses for everyone on the round, so it needs a lock that gets used. None of the A6, A8 or WH6 lists a fingerprint sensor or face unlock, so use a PIN or password. Set a lock on day one, and use whatever remote lock and wipe your account or device management software offers. Our product pages do not list voice calling, so if ringing customers from the device matters, ask us before you order.
