Almost every rugged tablet lists MIL-STD-810H on the spec sheet. It sits next to the IP rating as the badge that separates proper work kit from a consumer tablet in a chunky case. But most people buying one have no idea what was actually tested, and a surprising number of sellers do not either.

This guide explains what the standard is, what the famous drop test involves, what it does not cover, and how to tell a meaningful claim from a sticker.

What MIL-STD-810 Actually Is

MIL-STD-810 is a US Department of Defense standard for environmental testing. It describes how to test whether equipment survives the conditions it will meet in service: heat, cold, humidity, vibration, shock, dust, rain and so on. The H revision was published in 2019 and replaced 810G, which dated from 2008.

The important thing to understand is that MIL-STD-810 is a book of test methods, not a pass mark. It does not say "a tablet must survive X". It says "here is how to run a drop test, a heat test, a vibration test", and leaves the manufacturer to choose which tests are relevant and what limits to test against.

It Is Not a Certification (Whatever the Box Says)

You will often see phrases like "MIL-STD-810H certified". Strictly speaking, there is no such thing. No government body or official lab issues a MIL-STD-810 certificate for a consumer or trade device. The manufacturer runs the tests, or pays an independent lab to run them, and then declares that the device complies with whichever methods it chose.

That does not make the rating worthless. A device tested to the standard has been through a defined, repeatable procedure, which is a lot more than "drop tested" on its own means. But it does mean two tablets can both say MIL-STD-810H and have been through very different amounts of testing. One might have done the drop test only. Another might have done drop, vibration, temperature, humidity and dust.

The Drop Test in Detail

When people talk about MIL-STD-810 on a tablet, they almost always mean the drop test. It lives in Method 516 (Shock), under a procedure called the transit drop. For a hand carried item, the standard procedure is:

  • 26 drops, covering every face, every edge and every corner of the device
  • From 122cm (48 inches), roughly the height of a tablet slipping out of your hand while standing, or off a van tailgate
  • Onto 5cm plywood laid over concrete, not bare concrete
  • The device is checked for function after the drops

Two details are worth knowing. First, the plywood. It is a hard landing, but it is softer than a concrete slab, steel decking or a stone step. Second, the procedure allows the 26 drops to be shared across several test units rather than all hitting one device. Survival across the sequence is meaningful, but it is not the same as one tablet taking 26 hits in a row.

This is also why you see "1.2m drop tested" so often. That is the 48 inch figure from the standard. Claims of 1.5m or higher go beyond the standard procedure, which is fine if the manufacturer tested to that height, but ask what surface it landed on.

The Other Tests in the Standard

The drop test gets the attention, but the standard includes dozens of other methods. The ones that matter most for trade work are:

  • Method 501 and 502: High and low temperature. Does the device work, and survive storage, at the extremes? Relevant if your tablet lives in a van through a heatwave or a frost.
  • Method 503: Temperature shock. Rapid moves between hot and cold, like going from a heated cab to a freezing yard.
  • Method 507: Humidity. Long exposure to damp air, which is most of the UK for most of the year.
  • Method 510: Sand and dust. Blowing grit and fine particles. Overlaps with the first digit of the IP rating.
  • Method 514: Vibration. Hours of shaking, simulating transport or mounting on machinery. Matters if the tablet is mounted in a vehicle or on plant.
  • Method 509: Salt fog. Corrosion resistance. Worth asking about for coastal and marine work.

A device does not need to have passed every one of these to be a good work tablet. But if vibration matters to you because the tablet will be mounted on a digger, it is worth knowing whether it was tested for vibration, not just dropped.

810G vs 810H: Does the Letter Matter?

Not much, in practice. 810H tidied up and clarified a lot of the procedures in 810G, and some test details changed. But a tablet tested to 810G is not fragile, and a tablet tested to 810H is not indestructible. Which methods were run, and to what limits, tells you far more than the revision letter.

In our own range, the Tuga A6, Tuga A8, Tuga A10 and Tuga WH6 are listed to MIL-STD-810H. The Tuga W8 is listed to MIL-STD-810G. We would not pick between them on that basis alone.

What MIL-STD-810 Does Not Tell You

Even a thorough set of results has limits:

It says nothing about water ingress in the IP sense. The standard has a rain method, but the IP rating (IEC 60529) is the clearer guide to submersion and dust. Our guide to IP68 vs IP67 covers that side.

It does not mean the screen will never crack. Glass is glass. A drop onto a sharp corner of a kerb concentrates force in a way flat plywood does not. Rugged tablets survive far more than consumer ones, but a screen protector is still cheap insurance.

It does not cover wear over time. A device that passed 26 drops when new has not been tested for 26 drops a month for three years. Seals and bumpers age.

It does not cover your accessories. If you fit a third party case or mount, you have changed the device that was tested. Usually for the better, occasionally not.

Questions to Ask Before You Trust a Rugged Claim

If you are comparing devices, or a seller is leaning hard on the MIL-STD badge, these questions sort the real claims from the vague ones:

  1. Which test methods were run? Drop only, or drop plus others?
  2. What drop height, and onto what surface?
  3. Was the device tested bare, or in a case?
  4. Was the testing done in house or by an independent lab?
  5. Does the device also carry a proper IP rating (IP67 or IP68)?

A seller who can answer the first two clearly is usually being straight with you. A seller who cannot tell you anything beyond "it is military grade" is selling a phrase, not a test result.

The Bottom Line

MIL-STD-810H is a useful signal, not a guarantee. It tells you the manufacturer took durability seriously enough to test against a recognised procedure. Combine it with a genuine IP67 or IP68 rating, a screen you can read outdoors and a battery that lasts your shift, and you have the core of a proper work tablet.
