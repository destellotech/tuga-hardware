Three Jobs, Three Scanning Patterns

Stocktakes and cycle counts are hundreds or thousands of scans in a day, often by people who do not touch the device the rest of the year. They reward a device that scans fast, reads a dusty label from an awkward angle, and runs all day, because the count does not stop for a charger.

Goods in is bursty: twenty minutes of scanning cartons against a purchase order, then nothing for an hour. The device is picked up and put down a lot, often on a pallet or a racking beam, so sealing and a drop rating count for more than scan speed. A camera helps too, for photographing damaged cartons before you sign for them.

Picking is continuous: location barcode, item barcode, quantity, next line. The device is in the hand all shift, so weight and one-handed use matter most. A 277g handheld in a holster and a 910g tablet are very different things to carry for eight hours.

Why the Scanner Is the First Decision

Every device we sell can read a barcode with its camera, and for an occasional goods in check that is fine. Camera scanning is slower, needs the label in focus and lit, and asks for two hands while an app finds the code. Our guide to built-in barcode scanners compares the options in general terms. The short version for stock work: once scanning is the job, you want a dedicated engine and a physical key to fire it. There are two ways to get one: build it into the device, or add it alongside.

Option 2: A Plain Device and a Separate Scanner

If your scanning is light, say a goods in check twice a day and a monthly count, a built-in engine is nice but not essential, and a separate scanner alongside a plain tablet can be the cheaper route.

A Bluetooth scanner pairs with the tablet and types each scan into whatever field is active, the way a keyboard would, and most stock apps accept that without setup. One scanner can be shared between several tablets. Against that: two things to charge, a pairing that sometimes needs redoing, and a separate unit to leave on a pallet. We do not sell Bluetooth scanners, so we will not quote prices for them. Do your own sum: a plain Tuga A8 at £379 or Tuga A10 at £449 plus the scanner you choose, against £349 for an A6 with the engine already in it. For one user, the A6 usually wins. For a bench with a shared tablet, the separate scanner starts to make sense.

A wired USB scanner is the cheapest option of all if you already own one from a till or an old PC. Every Android device in our range has a single USB-C port, so a USB-A scanner needs the £8 USB-C to USB-A OTG Adapter. The WH6, W8 and W10 have a USB-A port and take it directly. A cable ties the scanner to the tablet, which suits a goods in bench far better than a picking round. Test that your app accepts the input before you commit.

The Rest of the Spec Sheet, in Stock Terms

Sealing and drops. Warehouses are dusty rather than wet, and the usual accident is a drop from a racking beam or a forklift step. The A6 and WH6 are rated for 1.5 metre drops, the W8 for 1.22 metres, and no drop figure is listed for the W10, A8 or A10. All of them are dust tight; IP67 on the A6 adds a rating for immersion, which IP65 on the Windows models does not.

Cold stores and dead spots. The A6 lists an operating range of -20°C to 50°C, the WH6 and W8 -20°C to 60°C; the W10, A8 and A10 list none, so ask before you take one into a chiller. Racking is hard on Wi-Fi, so walk the warehouse with the device on trial and find the dead spots before the count, not during it. The A6, WH6 and W8 have 4G built in as a fallback; the W10 lists it as an option.

NFC. Every Tuga model lists NFC. It is not a replacement for a barcode scanner, but it is a handy way to tag a bay, a cage or a returnable asset with a chip that reads with a tap even when the label has worn off. Our guide to what NFC on a work tablet is for explains where it fits.
