Who Needs a Wired Network Port

Wi-Fi covers most site work now, and a 4G SIM covers most of the rest. A wired Ethernet port is for the jobs where the equipment you are working on has no wireless at all, or where you are not allowed to use it.

Network and data installers configure switches, routers and access points before the Wi-Fi exists, over a cable to the management port. Security and fire system engineers commission panels and controllers designed to be reached over a wire from a laptop. Controls engineers plug into building management controllers the same way. Utilities and industrial engineers meet equipment where wireless is switched off by policy, or where the cabinet is a metal box in a basement with no signal.

In every one of those cases, the engineer carries a laptop for the cable. A tablet with an Ethernet port can replace it, if the software the job needs runs on the tablet. That second condition matters more than the port, so check it first: our guide to replacing a laptop with a rugged tablet covers what a tablet can and cannot take over.

The Tuga W10 lists 100Mbps Ethernet as standard, alongside dual-band Wi-Fi 5 and Bluetooth 5.2. That is slower than the gigabit port on a modern laptop. For configuring a controller, browsing a device's web page or pulling a log file, it is plenty. For moving very large files over the cable, it will take longer than a gigabit laptop, and you should know that before you buy. No other device in our range has a wired network port.

Who Needs HDMI

HDMI puts the tablet's screen on a bigger display. Site managers and surveyors can walk a client through drawings or a snagging list on the cabin TV rather than crowd around a 10 inch screen. Trainers can run toolbox talks from the tablet they already carry. An engineer commissioning in a plant room for two days can plug in a monitor and keyboard and work as if at a desk, then unplug and take the same device to the next site.

The Tuga W10 lists HDMI among its ports. The Tuga W8 lists Mini HDMI 2.0a, the smaller connector. It does the same job, but a standard HDMI cable will not fit it, so put a Mini HDMI to HDMI cable in the van before you need it. The WH6 and the three Android models list no HDMI or other display output, and their specifications do not list video over USB-C, so do not assume they can drive an external screen. If display output matters, buy the W10 or W8.

Who Needs USB-A

USB-A is the full size rectangular socket that most USB leads, memory sticks and dongles still use. USB-C is the smaller oval one. Phones and laptops have moved to USB-C, but a great deal of trade and industrial kit has not, and some of it never will.

The common cases are a USB lead from an instrument, a memory stick from a client or a machine that exports its logs to one, a wireless receiver for a keyboard or mouse, a wired barcode scanner or label printer, and a diagnostic interface between the tablet and a controller. If you do any of these regularly, a USB-A socket on the body saves you carrying and losing an adapter.

Three devices in our range have one built in. The Tuga W10 has USB-A and USB-C. The Tuga W8 has USB-A 3.0 and USB-C 3.1. The Tuga WH6 handheld has USB-A and a USB-C port that lists OTG support. The Tuga A6, Tuga A8 and Tuga A10 have USB-C only.

The £8 Adapter and What It Does Not Do

For the Android models, the answer to USB-A is the USB-C to USB-A OTG Adapter at £8 on our accessories page. It plugs into the USB-C port and gives you one USB-A socket, so a memory stick, a keyboard or a wired scanner can connect. It is listed as compatible with every device in the range, so it also serves as a second USB-A port on the Windows devices when the built-in one is taken.

Be clear about its limits before you rely on it:

  • One socket. A keyboard and a memory stick at the same time means a powered hub, which is another thing to carry and to check.
  • The tablet powers the device. Anything you plug in draws from the tablet's battery. A memory stick or a keyboard is fine. Something that expects more power, like a portable hard drive or a printer, may not run, and if it does it will cost you battery.
  • It occupies the charging port. On a USB-C only device, the adapter sits where the charger goes, so you cannot charge and use a USB-A device at the same time through it.
  • It is not a display adapter. It carries data, not video. It will not connect the A6, A8 or A10 to a monitor or projector.
  • It does not make software work. Memory sticks, keyboards and mice generally work because Android supports them. A manufacturer's USB interface for a boiler, a controller or an instrument usually needs that manufacturer's software, which may only exist for Windows. The adapter changes the shape of the plug, nothing else.

That last point is the one that catches people out. If your essential kit talks to Windows software over a USB lead, no adapter turns an Android tablet into the right tool. You need the W8, W10 or WH6, and even then you should check the manufacturer's supported platforms and try it on the device before you depend on it.

Ports and Sealing

Every socket is a hole in the case, sealed with a rubber flap or cover. Two things follow. First, the covers only work when they are closed: an IP65 tablet with its port flap open is not rated at all, so close it as soon as the cable comes out. Second, the devices with the most ports have the lower water rating. The W10, W8 and WH6 are IP65, which covers dust and water jets but not immersion. The A6 and A8 are IP67 and the A10 is IP68, both rated for immersion, and those are the devices with the fewest sockets. That is the trade-off this whole guide is about.
