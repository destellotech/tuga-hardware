Telecoms and fibre work is done standing in the road, half way up a pole or kneeling at an open chamber, usually with a van door open behind you and the weather doing whatever it likes. The device that holds your job pack, your serial scans and your photo evidence gets the same treatment as your tools.

What kills devices in telecoms and fibre work

  • Chambers and footway boxes. Standing water, silt and the grit that comes up with the lid. A phone balanced on the chamber edge goes in sooner or later.
  • Weather at the roadside. There is no shelter at a pole or a cabinet. Rain in autumn, frost on the cabinet door in winter and a screen washed out by low sun on a clear day.
  • Drops from height. Off a ladder or a pole platform onto tarmac, or out of a chest pocket while you lean into a cabinet.
  • Dust and fibre debris. Cabinet work, loft runs on customer installs and the dust from drilling through walls get into ports and speaker grilles.
  • Patchy signal. Rural cabinets and new build estates with no coverage yet mean forms and photos have to queue until the network comes back.

What actually matters in a spec

Sealing against water and grit. Look for IP67 or IP68, not "splash resistant". The first digit is dust, and a 6 means dust tight; the second is water, and a 7 means the device is rated for a metre of immersion for 30 minutes, which is the difference between a dropped phone in a chamber and a dropped phone you fish out and carry on with. Our IP68 vs IP67 guide explains the ratings in full.

A listed drop rating. A drop height or a MIL-STD-810 rating is a set of test conditions rather than a promise, but it means the device is designed to take drops a consumer screen would not. Check which devices actually list one: not all of ours do.

Scanning that does not depend on the camera. ONT serials, drop cable labels, splitter and tray tags: a built-in 2D scanner with a physical scan key reads them in the rain and with gloves on, where a camera app struggles. Our barcode scanner guide covers when a built-in engine earns its keep.

A camera with a flash. Before and after photos of a chamber, a cabinet or a loft run are what settle a dispute later. A flash matters more than megapixels in a dark footway box.

Pocket size and a removable battery. Up a pole, the device has to live in a pocket or a holster and come out one handed. A removable battery means a charged spare takes over on a long shift.

4G with a second SIM. Dual SIM lets you carry a second network for the estates where your main one has no coverage yet. Our connectivity guide covers when a SIM in the device beats hotspotting from a phone.

Windows only if a tool demands it. Most job management and survey apps run on Android or in a browser. Some test equipment and cabinet configuration software only runs on a Windows PC over USB; if yours does, that decides the device. Check your own tools before you buy.

Questions telecoms and fibre engineers ask

Will my job management or survey app run on it?

If the app runs in a web browser, or is on Google Play and supports the Android version on the device, it should. The A6 runs Android 15 and the A8 Android 12, so check the minimum version your app asks for. If your tools are Windows only, you need the W8 or another Windows model. Check with your software provider, and trial the app on the device before you rely on it.

Can I scan serials in the rain with gloves on?

The A6's scan engine is triggered by physical side keys, which work with gloves on, and the device is sealed to IP67, so rain is within the rating. Neither the A6 nor the A8 lists a glove mode for the touchscreen itself, so expect to take a glove off to type.

What about the cold?

The A6 is rated for -20°C to 50°C. Batteries lose capacity in the cold and recover when warm, which is why a removable spare kept in an inside pocket is the practical answer on a winter shift. Our cold weather battery guide explains what to expect.
