Solar and EV work moves a tablet through more conditions in a day than most trades manage in a week: a roof in full sun, a hot loft, a garage wall for the charge point, then the kitchen table for the forms and photos your schemes and network operator ask for. A consumer tablet copes with one of those. The combination breaks it.

What kills devices in solar and EV installation

  • Sun on the roof. A dim screen at midday means you cannot read the layout drawing or the commissioning app, so you cup a hand over it or go back down the ladder.
  • Heat. A device left face up on a roof or a van dashboard in July gets hot enough to dim its screen or refuse to charge. Summer lofts are worse.
  • Loft dust and insulation fibres. Routing DC cable through a loft fills the air with dust and loose mineral wool that gets into ports and under case edges.
  • Rain and wet hands. Heavy rain stops roof work, but drizzle, wet tiles and a wet scaffold do not stop the paperwork, and driveway charge point installs happen in all weathers.
  • Drops from height. Off a roof ladder, off the scaffold boards, off the stepladder in the loft hatch. Rarely survivable for a tablet in a soft case.

What actually matters in a spec

Brightness, in nits. This is the spec most installers under-buy. Anything described as "sunlight readable" without a number is a guess. Our guide to nits explains the figures; for roof work you want the high hundreds, not the low.

Sealing against dust and water. IP67 or IP68 means dust tight and rated for immersion, which covers drizzle, wet gloves and loft dust comfortably. The difference between the two is in our IP68 vs IP67 guide.

A listed drop rating. A drop height or a MIL-STD-810H rating is a set of test conditions, not a promise, but it means the device was designed for the fall. Not every rugged device lists one, including one of ours. Our roofers and scaffolders guide covers drops and straps for work at height.

A camera with a flash. Handover packs and scheme submissions live on photos: labels, isolator positions, the charge point in place. Lofts and garages are dark, so a flash matters more than megapixels.

Mobile data. Commissioning apps often want a connection while you set equipment up, and the customer's Wi-Fi rarely reaches the garage or the loft. Built-in 4G with two SIM slots gives you a second network on a rural roof.

A full day of battery, or a spare. Commissioning runs long. A removable battery means a charged spare takes over instead of a hunt for the kitchen socket.

Android is enough. Inverter, battery and charge point apps are published for phones and tablets, and the portals run in a browser; design software that needs Windows stays in the office. Our Android or Windows guide has a five minute test.

Questions solar and EV installers ask

Will the inverter, battery and charge point apps run on it?

If the app is published for Android and supports the version on the device, it should. The A8 runs Android 12, the A10 Android 14 and the A6 Android 15, so check each app's minimum version; a newer app is more likely to leave Android 12 behind. We do not test individual apps on our devices, so try yours before you rely on it, and use the 30-day returns if it does not suit.

Can I read it on the roof?

The A8's 1000 nit screen is built for it. The A10 at 350 nits is not a roof screen in direct sun, and the A6 lists no brightness figure, so treat it as a pocket device for scanning and photos rather than for reading drawings in sunlight. Our nits guide has the full picture.

What about photos for the handover pack?

All three have a 13MP rear camera with a flash, and the A8's is autofocus. Photograph labels before the panels go up and the finished install before you leave; our guide to site photos that hold up covers framing, light in lofts and backups.
