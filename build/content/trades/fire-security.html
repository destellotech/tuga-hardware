Fire and security work is paperwork with a torch in the other hand. Every detector, call point, camera and reader has a record, every commissioning visit has a test to log, and most of the devices are somewhere dark, dusty or high. A consumer tablet copes with the reception desk and gives up in the riser.

What kills devices in fire and security work

  • Ceiling voids and risers. Lifting tiles brings down years of dust and insulation fibre. The tablet sits on the top of the steps or on a cable tray while you work, and the ports fill with whatever is up there.
  • Plant rooms and roof spaces. Damp, hot in summer, cold in winter, and a long way from a socket. The device that holds the device schedule has to survive the same conditions as the panel.
  • Drops from steps. One hand on a detector base, one on the tablet, and the tablet is the one that goes. A fall from the top of a stepladder onto a tiled corridor is how most screens end.
  • Long commissioning days. Walking a building testing every device, with the schedule open all day and the camera busy, drains a battery that was designed for an evening on the sofa.
  • Two devices per job. A phone for photos, a tablet for the records, and a laptop in the van for the panel. Three batteries, three places for a photo to end up, and a customer waiting for a signature.

What actually matters in a spec

Sealing against dust first. Look for IP67 or IP68. The first digit covers dust and a 6 means dust tight, which is the figure that matters above a ceiling. IP65 is also dust tight but only rated against water jets, not immersion, which is fine for most indoor security work. Our IP68 vs IP67 guide explains the ratings.

A camera that works in the dark. A photo of a detector label, a panel's wiring or a camera's field of view is often taken in a void with no light. A rear camera with a flash matters more here than megapixels. Our guide to site photos that hold up covers how to shoot and file them.

Enough screen for a device schedule. A schedule of a few hundred devices with zone, loop, address, type and test result is a spreadsheet. On a phone it is a scroll; on an 8 inch screen it is a page.

A battery you can swap. Commissioning does not stop at lunchtime. A removable battery means a charged spare in the toolbag rather than a search for a socket in a corridor. See which devices have one in our guide to removable batteries.

NFC for asset tags. Every Tuga model lists NFC. If your maintenance platform supports tagging devices or check-in points, a tap replaces typing an address. Our guide to what NFC is actually for says what it can and cannot do.

Android for the apps, Windows only for the panel. Job management, service records and most maintenance apps run on Android or in a browser. Panel configuration software from some manufacturers is Windows only and connects by USB or a serial lead. If that is your daily tool, you need a Windows device with a full size USB port; if it is an occasional one, the laptop in the van may still be the right answer. Our Android or Windows guide has a five minute test for this.

Questions fire and security engineers ask

Will my service and job management app run on it?

If it runs in a browser, or has an Android version that supports Android 12 on the A8 or Android 15 on the A6, it should. We publish pages matching the published device requirements of apps common in this trade, including Joblogic, Simpro and BigChange, to our devices, with sources, under works with. None of them is a claim that we have tested the app on the device, so trial it on one unit before you equip the team.

Can I keep the certificates and records my schemes require on it?

Any app that produces them will store or sync them like any other document. Set the device up so records go to a business account rather than the engineer's own, with a screen lock and remote wipe turned on, because a lost tablet full of a client's alarm layouts is a bigger problem than the tablet. Our guide to protecting customer data on a work tablet walks through it.

Can I use it with gloves on?

None of the three lists a glove mode for the touchscreen, so try your own gloves and expect to take one off at times. The A6's side scan keys work with gloves on, and the stylus helps on the A8 and W8.

Is it worth it over a cheap tablet in a case?

A case helps with drops and does nothing for dust in the ports or a long day without a socket. The sums are in rugged vs consumer tablet cost.
